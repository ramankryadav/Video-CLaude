## Cold open · The metal that paid the world
[natural pace] [bold] This is silver. [0.3s] You probably think it's gold's cheaper little sibling, the metal for spoons, anklets and second place medals. [0.4s] But for most of human history, silver, not gold, was the money that ordinary people actually used.
[sarcasm] A lucky strike of silver built the warships that saved ancient Athens. [0.3s] A mountain of it in South America built a city as big as London, and killed thousands of the people who mined it. [serious] Silver conducts electricity better than any other metal on Earth, and it reflects light better than any other metal too. [0.3s] That's why it's inside your phone, your car and almost every solar panel. [0.5s] And remember this, because unlike gold, a huge share of the silver we dig up is used once, and then it's gone for good.
[interest] It wasn't silver's beauty that made it so powerful, and it wasn't its rarity. [0.3s] It was usefulness, first as money in every market, and now as a metal the modern world can't run without. ## Forged in exploding stars
[natural pace] [awe] Silver is older than the Earth itself. [0.3s] Scientists believe it was forged inside dying stars and in some of the most violent explosions in the universe, from giant stars blowing apart to dead neutron stars smashing together. [0.4s] Some astronomers even think silver and gold were mostly made in different kinds of cosmic events.
[thoughtful] About four and a half billion years ago, those atoms became part of the new Earth. [0.3s] Today, silver is nearly twenty times more common than gold in the Earth's crust, but it's still rare, about one gram in thirteen tonnes of rock. [0.4s] And unlike gold, silver rarely sits around in pure nuggets, which is why humans found it so much later.

## Hidden inside lead (3500 BC)
[natural pace] [curiosity] Most of the world's silver is hidden inside other ores, especially lead. [0.3s] More than five thousand years ago, in what's now Turkey, Syria and Iran, metalworkers figured out how to get it out. [0.4s] They melted the ore and blew air over it, until the lead burned away and a tiny bead of shining silver was left behind.
[interest] The process is called cupellation, and archaeologists have found its waste, a lead residue called litharge, at sites more than five thousand years old. [0.3s] It was dirty, toxic and slow work. [0.4s] But it meant that, for the first time, people could make silver wherever they could find the right rock.

## The first money (1750 BC)
[natural pace] [serious] In ancient Mesopotamia, the land between the Tigris and Euphrates rivers, silver became something new, money. [0.3s] There were no coins yet, so people weighed out pieces of silver, using a unit called the shekel. [0.4s] Rings, coils and scraps of silver were cut and weighed to pay for grain, land and debts.
[authoritative] Around 1750 before Christ, King Hammurabi of Babylon carved nearly three hundred laws into stone, many with prices in silver. [0.3s] According to one law, a hired labourer earned about one shekel of silver a month. [0.4s] A doctor who performed a successful operation could charge ten shekels, so silver was already setting prices almost four thousand years ago.

## The silver pharaoh (1000 BC)
[natural pace] [surprised] In Egypt, gold came fairly easily from the mines of Nubia, but silver had to be brought in from far away. [0.3s] So for long stretches of Egyptian history, silver was reportedly valued even more highly than gold. [0.4s] Egyptians said the gods had flesh of gold, and bones of silver.

[0.3s] Inside, the king lay in a coffin made of silver, with a gold mask on his face. [0.5s] But the world was at war, and one of the greatest royal burials ever found barely made the news.

## The silver that saved Athens (483 BC)

[surprised] In 480 before Christ, the huge Persian fleet was lured into the narrow straits of Salamis, and the Greek ships crushed it. [0.3s] Athens went on to its golden age, stamping its famous owl coins from the same mines. ## Rome's silver habit (200 BC to 270 AD)
[natural pace] [serious] Rome took the silver mines of Spain from Carthage, and worked them on a massive scale. ## The valley that named the dollar (1516)
[natural pace] [curiosity] Fast forward to 1516, in a valley called Joachimsthal, in what's now the Czech Republic. [0.3s] Miners found rich silver there, and a local count began minting big silver coins called Joachimsthalers. [0.4s] The name was a mouthful, so people shortened it to thaler.

[0.3s] American colonists used that word for the Spanish silver coins they handled every day, and after independence, the United States made it the name of its own money. ## The mountain that eats men (1545)
[natural pace] [curiosity] In 1545, high in the Andes, in what's now Bolivia, a local man named Diego Huallpa made a discovery. [0.3s] According to a famous story, he lit a fire on a mountainside, and melted silver began to trickle out of the ground. [natural pace] [excited] In 1859, miners in the hills of Nevada kept cursing a heavy blue mud that clogged their gold equipment. [0.3s] Then someone tested it, and it turned out to be incredibly rich silver ore. [0.4s] It was the Comstock Lode, the first major silver discovery in the United States.

[natural pace] [serious] For centuries, many countries used both gold and silver as money, at a fixed ratio. [natural pace] [interest] In 1839, Louis Daguerre unveiled one of the first practical forms of photography, made on silver-coated copper plates. [natural pace] [cautious] Meanwhile, silver was disappearing from people's pockets.
